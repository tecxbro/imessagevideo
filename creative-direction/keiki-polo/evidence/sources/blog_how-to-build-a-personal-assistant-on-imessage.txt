How to Build a Personal Assistant on iMessage
Build an AI personal assistant on iMessage that books flights, handles email, gathers tax documents and runs errands, with or without code. Eleven steps, from the line to payments.
It's Sunday night and you text: "find me the cheapest nonstop to Austin thursday morning, aisle seat, under $400." Four minutes later: "United 1142, 7:05am, aisle 14C, $312. Want me to book it?" You reply "yes." The confirmation number arrives a minute later, the flight is on your calendar, and your Monday 9am has moved to 11, because you'll be in the air.
On Tuesday you text "my accountant needs my 1099s." It finds three in your inbox, sends them over, and tells you one is still missing from your brokerage. Your Wednesday starts with a short message listing your meetings, the bill that's due and a heads-up that it'll rain.
That's a personal assistant on iMessage. There's no app to install and nothing new to learn. It lives in the thread you already open dozens of times a day, it remembers what you told it months ago, and it does real work in your accounts instead of just talking about it.

What is a personal assistant on iMessage?
It's an AI agent with its own phone number that works for one person. You text it the way you'd text a capable friend who has your calendar open and your card in their wallet. It reads what you send, including photos and voice notes, decides what to do, uses your email, calendar, the web and a payment card, and replies in the same thread.
Two things separate it from a chatbot. It works between your messages, so it can check in on a schedule, watch for a price drop or keep going on a task after you've put your phone down. And it knows the difference between what it may do alone and what needs your yes first.
iMessage is a natural home for it. It's where people already make plans, and a text needs no download, no login screen and no permission prompt.
What can a personal assistant on iMessage do?
More than most people expect. If you'd hand a task to a trusted human assistant with access to your inbox, your calendar, a browser and a card, a texted assistant can usually do it too, with your approval before it spends money or speaks for you.
Travel.
 Find and book flights and hotels, check you in, watch for gate changes, and rebook when a flight is cancelled.
Email.
 Triage the inbox overnight, flag what's urgent, draft replies in your voice and chase people who haven't answered.
Calendar.
 Find a time that works for four people, move meetings when plans change, and brief you before each call.
Money.
 Pay a bill, cancel a subscription you forgot about, chase a refund, and tell you where your money went this month.
Taxes and paperwork.
 Collect your 1099s and receipts from your inbox, remind you before estimated tax deadlines, and send your accountant what they asked for.
Errands.
 Order groceries, buy a gift, start a return, book the dentist, reserve a table for six.
Home and family.
 Track packages, fill in school forms, remember the plumber's name and when the filter needs changing.
Memory.
 "What was the wine we liked at Dana's?" three months later, answered in one line.
The common thread: each task needs a mix of the same few abilities, which is why the build comes down to a handful of parts.

The seven parts of a personal assistant
Every texted assistant is built from the same seven parts:
A line.
 An iMessage number it sends from and receives on, online around the clock.
Memory.
 Durable facts about the person ("vegetarian", "Dana is my manager", "I fly United") recalled when they matter.
Connected accounts.
 Each person signs in to their own email and calendar, and the assistant uses that account and nobody else's.
A schedule.
 Briefings, reminders and watches that run without a message first.
A browser.
 For the large share of life that has no API: airline sites, utility portals, booking forms.
A way to pay.
 A card it can spend with, under limits you set, without ever seeing the number.
Approvals.
 A clear line between what it does alone and what waits for a yes.
You can build each part yourself, and the step-by-step guide below explains what each one takes. Or you can start with all seven already in place.

How to build a personal assistant on iMessage, step by step
Each step starts with the decision you have to make, whatever you build on, and then shows how we do it on Keiki, with code from our TypeScript SDK. You can do every step in the dashboard instead; the code just makes each choice visible.
Step 1: Decide what it's for
The fastest way to build a bad assistant is to start with "it does everything." Look at the list above and pick three jobs you'd actually hand off this week. A morning briefing, inbox triage and travel booking is a strong start. Each job tells you which parts you need: a briefing needs a calendar and a schedule, travel needs a browser and a card.
Then write two short lists. The first is what the assistant may do on its own: read, summarize, remind, search, draft. The second is what it must ask about first: send anything as you, spend money, delete anything, accept an invitation. These lists become the rules in Step 3 and the approvals in Step 9, and they'll save you from the most common way personal assistants lose people's trust.
Step 2: Get an iMessage line
Apple has no public bot API for an ordinary phone number, so every assistant on iMessage runs on a real iMessage account somewhere. There are three realistic ways to get one:
A Mac you run.
 Sign a Mac in to an Apple ID and run a bridge such as 
BlueBubbles
. Fine for a personal project, but the Mac has to stay awake and signed in, and every update or power cut is an outage.
A line provider.
 Companies such as Linq and Photon run lines and give you an API and webhooks. You still build everything above the line: grouping bursts of messages, retries, delivery status and history.
A platform that does both.
 The line and the agent runtime come together, so the line is just a setting.
Whichever you pick, treat the line with care. Apple publishes no sending rules, and lines that blast cold messages get flagged. Let people text the assistant first, keep conversations two-way, and never send a burst. Our 
iMessage best practices
 cover this in detail, and

Step 3: Write its personality and rules
The system prompt is where the assistant gets a voice and where your two lists become rules. For a personal assistant, four things matter most:
Sound like a text.
 Short replies, no headings, no bullet-point essays. An assistant that sends four paragraphs gets muted.
Know the time and the timezone.
 "Tomorrow morning" means nothing without them.
Spell out what needs a yes.
 Copy your second list straight in.
Be honest about access.
 If it isn't sure how it knows something, it should say so. The Muse story is what happens when an assistant improvises an explanation.

Step 4: Give it a memory
An assistant needs two kinds of memory. The thread is short-term memory: the last few dozen messages, passed to the model on every turn. Long-term memory is a set of durable facts pulled out of conversations and recalled when they're relevant, so "I'm vegetarian" in March still shapes the restaurant it books in October, and "I always want an aisle seat" never has to be said twice.
The usual pattern is to extract facts after each turn and retrieve the relevant ones before the next. The hard part is deciding what's worth keeping. Preferences, people, places, routines and account details ("my frequent flyer number is…") are. One-off moments ("I'm running late") mostly aren't. Sort facts into a few categories so retrieval stays focused, and keep each person's memory strictly apart from everyone else's.

Step 5: Connect their accounts
Email and calendar are where an assistant becomes indispensable, and where homemade ones most often cut corners. The rules are simple to state:
Each person signs in to their own account.
 Use OAuth, and send the sign-in link in the thread the first time it's needed.
Tokens belong to that person.
 Never share one across users, and never put one in the prompt or anywhere the model can read it.
Start with what the jobs need.
 Reading a calendar covers a morning briefing. Add sending and editing when a job calls for it.
Notice when access is revoked.
 People disconnect apps. Check that a token still works, and ask them to reconnect when it doesn't.
On Keiki:

Step 6: Let it text first
An assistant that only answers is half an assistant. The morning briefing, the "your flight boards in two hours," the "that jacket dropped to $120": all of it comes from a scheduler that runs a prompt for each person at the right time. Three details make it welcome instead of annoying:
Use their timezone.
 7:40 should mean 7:40 where they are.
Stay quiet when there's nothing to say.
 An empty day doesn't need a briefing.
Remember the last run.
 "Tell me if the price drops" only works if the assistant knows what the price was last time.
Keep these messages rare and useful. Every proactive text spends a little of the person's attention, and on iMessage a little of the line's reputation.

Step 7: Give it a browser
Most of life has no API. Airline sites, utility portals, a restaurant's booking form, the page where you cancel a subscription: a personal assistant that can't use them can only tell you what to do. A real browser lets it do it.
Use two tools. Search and page reading answer quick questions cheaply ("is the pharmacy open?"). A full browser session handles tasks with several steps, like comparing fares and filling in a booking. Run that browser in the cloud, never on the person's own machine, and give each task its own session.
And keep one hard rule: the assistant never types the person's password or solves their CAPTCHA. When it reaches a login or a check only a human should pass, it hands the live page to the person, they finish that one step, and the assistant carries on.

Step 8: Let it pay
Booking the flight is the point, not finding it. To finish a task that costs money, the assistant needs a way to pay, and this is the step to get right before anything else in this guide reaches real people.
Three rules hold up:
The model never sees a card number.
 It asks to spend; something outside the model decides and pays.
Every purchase is checked against limits.
 A cap per purchase, daily and monthly caps, and an approval threshold above which a person decides.
The card matches the purchase.
 Ideally a one-time card for exactly the approved amount, so a mistake or a manipulated page can't spend more.
On Keiki:

agent wallet

Agentcard

The browser fills it in at checkout, and the model never sees the number. The Instinct template is built around this flow: "book it" gets an exact total back, and nothing is charged until the person approves it.
Step 9: Put risky actions behind approval
Payments aren't the only thing that should wait. Sort every tool into three buckets. Reads run on their own. Writes you can undo, like adding a to-do or a calendar hold, can usually run too. Anything that leaves the thread and can't be taken back, such as an email sent as the person, a cancelled reservation or an accepted invitation, waits for a yes.
The pattern that works best over text is draft, then confirm. The assistant shows the email or the change in the thread, the person replies "send it," and only then does it happen. Log every action, so "why did you do that?" always has an answer.

Step 10: Handle how people actually text
People don't text in tidy single messages. They send "hey", "can you move my dentist" and "to thursday" as three texts. They add "actually friday" while the assistant is still answering. They send voice notes from the car and photos of a parking sign or a receipt. An assistant that answers each fragment separately feels broken, however smart the model is.
So wait a moment after a message and gather a burst into one turn. Get messages that arrive mid-answer into the turn that's already running. Transcribe audio and describe images before the model sees them. And speak the thread's language: a thumbs-up tapback that says "done" beats a sentence that says the same thing.

FAQ
Can I build a personal assistant on iMessage without code?
Yes. Sign up for Keiki, start from the Orchid or Instinct template, edit its instructions in plain words and add an iMessage line. With Orchid, people connect their own Gmail, Google Calendar or Outlook from a link the assistant texts them, so there's nothing to code.
Can a personal assistant on iMessage book flights and pay for things?
Yes, with the right setup. It needs a browser to search and fill in the booking, and a wallet that pays with a one-time card for exactly the approved amount. On Keiki the browser runs on Browserbase and payments run on Agentcard, and nothing is charged until the person approves the total.
Do I need a Mac to run one?
No. On a hosted iMessage line nothing in your stack needs macOS. Keiki supplies dedicated lines and keeps them online and delivering.
Is there an official iMessage API for personal assistants?
No. Apple has no public server API for bots on an ordinary phone number, and Apple Messages for Business is a separate channel for verified brands. Assistants like this run on hosted lines. Our guide to 
the iMessage API
 explains the options.
How does a texted assistant get into my email and calendar?
Through per-user OAuth. The assistant texts you a sign-in link, you sign in to your own account, and the credential stays with you. On Keiki it's never shared with other users or shown to the model.
Can it text me first?
Yes. Loops run on a cron or interval schedule, such as a weekday morning briefing, and quiet loops send nothing when there's nothing to say. Keiki also limits how many new conversations a line can start each day, because conversations people start themselves are much safer for a line.
Can the same assistant work on WhatsApp?
Yes. On Keiki channels sit below the assistant, so the same assistant, memory and connected accounts can answer on iMessage, WhatsApp, SMS, Telegram, Slack and email.
